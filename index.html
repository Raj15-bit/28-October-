The gains sat in power gear, pipes, defence and chemicals, the same places where order books are growing. That fits a market that buys "things India must build" and sells "things AI may replace".

3 pulls on Indian money at once

  • Jio's share sale — the biggest Indian IPO (initial public offering, a company's first sale of shares to the public) took bids of ₹7.4 lakh crore. A small dip in shares on 27 October was blamed on cash being parked for it.
  • Foreign funds — they bought ₹4,240 crore of Indian shares in cash on 27 October, their biggest day of the month, while Indian funds sold.
  • Software fear — Anthropic's IPO filing showed how fast an AI company can grow, and Indian IT shares fell on the worry that AI will take their work.
  • Rates — RBI raised its policy rate on 9 October, and lenders now pass that on from 1 November.

What ties the day together

Reading between the lines, the same thing is pushing money everywhere: capital has become scarce and expensive. AI builders raised about $200 billion in a single day. US-friendly countries are buying far fewer US government bonds. India is raising rates and, by a news report, thinking about limiting how many dollars people can send abroad.

One view is that this is a good set-up for Indian companies with real orders, because profits are growing at the fastest pace in years. The opposite view is that dear money and dear oil always reach profits with a delay, and that the drought will hit rural demand by the winter. Section 10 sets these arguments side by side.

02

The price of money is rising, at home and abroad

RBI's turn, loans racing ahead of deposits, dollars leaving, and US bond rates above 5%

In short
  • India has started raising interest rates again, and lenders are passing it on.
  • Loans are growing much faster than a year ago, so banks need deposits badly.
  • Abroad, money is just as dear: US bond rates are above 5% and friendly countries buy fewer US bonds.

RBI turned from cutting to raising

RBI raised its repo rate (the rate at which it lends to banks) by 0.25% to 5.50% on 9 October, its first rise since February 2023, and said its stance is now "calibrated tightening". In July, company calls were about enjoying rate cuts. By October they were about rate rises.

  • More to come — lenders on their calls expect about 2 more rises.
  • Loan rates — non-bank lenders raise their prime lending rate by 0.20% to 0.25% from 1 November; cost of funds is about 7.33%.
  • Bond losses — IndiaMART and Maruti booked losses on bonds they hold, because bond prices fall when rates rise.
  • Bank warning — the head of Axis Bank said tougher times lie ahead: less spare cash in the system, dearer deposits and thinner margins, even though loan demand is strong.

Loans are running ahead of deposits

Bank loans grow faster than deposits, so banks must pay more to attract savings. That is why deposit rates rise and why a state bank like Indian Overseas Bank plans to raise ₹5,000 crore of new capital.

Government banks did better than private ones in the September quarter. They grew interest income and profit faster from the June quarter, helped by cheaper deposits and lower bad-loan costs.

One view is that higher rates will cool this credit boom quickly. The opposite view is that loan demand is so strong that rates will mostly squeeze bank margins rather than slow lending. IDFC First Bank's chief already talks of a ₹10 lakh crore loan book.

Keeping dollars at home

A TV channel reported that RBI has asked the government to tighten the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS, the rule that lets a person send up to $250,000 abroad a year). The reported idea ends investment outflows on the automatic route and caps spending abroad. RBI has not announced this, and a search found no official confirmation.

  • For a curb — rich Indians, not foreign funds, are taking the dollars out, so the rule targets the right people.
  • Against — LRS is already fully in RBI's hands; it should stop changing, and checks should apply only above ₹5 crore.
  • Open question — employees who receive shares from foreign employers (ESPPs) asked whether they would be hit.

Reading between the lines, a weak rupee and money leaving at the same time push governments toward capital controls. One view says these are coming everywhere: dollar deposit schemes, travel taxes and remittance limits are all signs of it.

A related point came up too: measured in dollars, India's income has grown only about as fast as America's or China's over the past decade, despite faster real growth, because the rupee keeps falling. Brazil is the warning: its income per person jumped in the commodity boom, then stalled for 12 years.

  • Income per person, India — about $469 (2002), $1,540 (2014), $2,750 (2026); China went from $1,160 to $15,370 over the same years.

The world's cost of money

  • US bonds — the 10-year yield (what lenders demand to lend to the US government for 10 years) is back above 5%.
  • Fewer buyers — US-friendly countries, led by Asia, cut purchases of US government bonds by about 60% from 2025, to the lowest since 2021.
  • Federal Reserve — a hold at 3.75% to 4.00% was expected on 28 October under its new chair, Kevin Warsh; markets priced 82% to 90% odds of a rise in December. Goldman Sachs expected exactly that path.
  • Bank of Canada — raised its rate to 2.50%, its first rise since July 2023, warning that energy prices can spread into general inflation.
  • New Zealand — the central bank surprised by holding at 2.75% when a rise was priced in; its currency fell about 1.9%.
  • Japan and Australia — Japan's underlying inflation hit 3.2%, a 3-year high; Australia's core inflation was 3.7%, so a rise there is over 80% priced.
  • Bonds as a shield — US bond yields and the Nasdaq moved together again (a correlation of +0.51), so bonds once more rise when tech shares fall.

One view explains the bond selling simply: when oil or the dollar is too high, foreign countries run short of dollars and sell US bonds to pay their bills. Jamie Dimon of JPMorgan made a blunter point, that a country pulled its gold out of New York because "reserve status" rests on military and economic strength.

Tax and market rules

  • Capital gains tax — a plea to cut long-term capital gains tax for everyone met the view that cuts should favour asset-building (REITs, InvITs and bank deposits) instead; another worry is that the cut may come only for foreign funds.
  • STT — active traders want the securities transaction tax gone because it cannot be set off against gains.
  • SEBI — may relax the extra disclosure rules for foreign funds that hold a lot in a single group, which began after the Hindenburg report in 2023.
  • GST — brokerage paid by foreign funds and NRIs from repatriable accounts now counts as an export, so it carries no GST; Jefferies called this good for brokers.
  • GST registration — CBIC set 7-day approval for Aadhaar-checked applications, and tax demands now need evidence; prosecution starts only above ₹5 crore.
03

Oil, ships and war: peace talk on paper, a dear barrel in fact

Iran's terms, the threat of a short new war, record ship costs, and who now buys Russian oil

In short
  • Iran and its mediators talk of a deal, but oil stays near its high because the US is preparing for a short new war after its elections.
  • Shipping, not the oil itself, is the main cost now: hiring a supertanker costs about $1.3 million a day.
  • India is a winner in refining and a loser as a buyer, and companies are raising prices.

Deal talk and war plans at the same time

Iran's Supreme Leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, called for conditions to end the war. A 7-day plan was floated: Iran reopens the Strait of Hormuz (the narrow sea lane for about a fifth of the world's oil) on day 6 if the US accepts, and talks begin on day 7.

  • Qatar's draft — mediators gave Iran a new draft deal with daily steps and guarantor countries; a senior Iranian official said no new proposal had been made and called the ideas unacceptable.
  • US plans — the Wall Street Journal reported that the US told Gulf states to prepare for a "short, intense" campaign after the 3 November midterm elections; more warships and Patriot missiles are moving.
  • Attacks — Houthis claimed hits on Saudi Arabia's Ras Tanura refinery (550,000 barrels a day) and its defence ministry; Saudi Arabia said it stopped 2 missiles and the refinery fire was contained.
  • Mines — Iran said a tanker caught fire after hitting mines east of Hormuz, after a US commander flew over the strait and called it clear.
  • Nuclear sites — the IAEA found no sign Iran is rebuilding them, but its inspectors are kept out.

One view from oil traders is that "daily confidence steps" are words, and each such headline moves oil by less than $1. The opposite view is that the market underrates a deal, because Iran, Turkey, Saudi Arabia, Qatar and Pakistan are already meeting in Tehran. The ship, not the barrel

Ships are being hit, so owners demand huge fees to sail. About 119 ships have been damaged this year. Freight now adds about $33 to each barrel, almost a third of its price.

  • Oil spills — a hit supertanker leaked off Umm Al Quwain; the UAE shut beaches, and the slick stretched over 22 miles. A supertanker holds about 8 times the oil the Exxon Valdez spilled.
  • Sinokor — Iran's new strait authority started "legal action" against the world's biggest tanker owner, and at least 7 ships faced enforcement.
  • Saudi rush — Saudi Arabia is loading about 9 supertankers at once and sent over 7 million barrels a day in early October, near pre-war levels; one reading is "sell while you can" before a new war.
  • Aramco — will store crude abroad, at Singapore's Jurong caverns from 2027, a break from its old rule of keeping oil at home.

Reading between the lines, a spill near a desalination plant (a plant that turns sea water into drinking water) is a bigger risk than lost oil. Gulf states depend on it for most of their water, and the UAE keeps only about 2 days of stored water.

  • Ukraine under fire — 16 ballistic missiles hit Kyiv in 1 night and only 2 were stopped; Ukraine needs about 300 Patriot interceptors.
  • US and Russian oil — reports say the US may return to Russian oil and diesel; Trump said 3-way peace talks may not happen in person.

Europe's gas and America's fuel

  • Europe gas — TTF passed €100 per megawatt-hour; storage is about 75% full against a normal 90%, and Goldman Sachs sees €150 if Gulf gas stays cut and winter is cold.
  • Refinery closures — the European Commission called them a "wake-up call" but will only act early next year.
  • US stocks of fuel — crude stocks fell for a 4th week; US output hit a record; WTI rose back above $102.
  • US petrol — average prices under Trump's 2nd term passed those under Biden, days before the elections.
  • Refiners gain — Phillips 66 earned a refining margin of $38 a barrel and ran its plants at 98%.

India is now the 2nd-largest seaborne diesel exporter after the US, and it will add the most refining capacity in the world by the end of 2027. The same war that raises India's import bill pays its refiners.

  • Price rises — Tata Consumer's chief said more price rises are likely if crude goes above $115; without the energy shock, its India food margin would be 17% to 20% instead of 14%.

One view is that Jio is still cheap for what it owns: about half of India's mobile broadband, most of its data traffic, and the power to raise prices. Brokers who back it set targets close to the listing price.

India's economy: a dry year, careful shoppers, and fuller trains

The drought, card spending's first fall, what shoppers still buy, and how people move

In short
  • The monsoon failed, so food prices and rural loans are the main worries for the winter.
  • People still spend on beer, premium drinks and quick delivery, but credit card spending fell for the first time.
  • New trains and roads are filling up fast, while air travel is shrinking.

A drought year

Do factory subsidies work?

Andy Mukherjee of Bloomberg Opinion argued that 1 in 4 iPhones is now made in India, but with imported parts and costly subsidies, so India should stop paying production-linked incentives (PLI, cash for each unit made). China's Global Times quoted the piece with approval.

One view, the defence of the scheme, says China also began by assembling. A newer scheme, ECMS, pays only for parts, at falling rates and only after a plant is checked. The opposite view says assembly profits are tiny and the hard parts, chips and the machines that make things, are still imported.

  • ECMS so far — outlay ₹40,000 crore, ₹0 paid yet, 106 plants approved and 38 already producing.
  • Parts now made here — displays (Samsung Display, Noida), camera modules (Q Tech, Kaynes), circuit boards (12 makers incl. Kaynes, AT&S, Syrma), battery cells (ATL), casings (Tata Electronics, Motherson, Foxconn's Yuzhan). Memory and processor chips are still imported.
  • Government numbers — Ashwini Vaishnaw said ₹19,000 crore of mobile PLI was paid, against ₹25,000 crore of direct tax and ₹3 lakh crore of GST from the sector, which employs 25 lakh people.
  • Next scheme — a new ₹62,500 crore mobile manufacturing scheme runs from FY27 to FY31.
  • The history — on the 2009 iPhone 3G, China's assembler earned $6.50 out of a $178.96 build cost; China's share of iPhone value later grew to about 25%.
  • China's pushback — in 2025 China slowed exports of special equipment to India and recalled engineers from Indian lines.
